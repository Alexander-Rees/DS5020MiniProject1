# Customer segments (ML6): analysis for marketing

Source: `reports/segments.csv`, `reports/segment_profiles.json`, `figures/customer_map.png` and the supplementary
`figures/customer_map_components.png`, regenerated by
`python model_analysis.py`. Everything here is computed from **training** purchases (up to 2011-10-09) of the
5,473 customers in the matrix. No customer IDs are used or shown.

## How the groups were made

1. The chosen SVD (k = 100, log purchase value) gives each customer a score on every component. The map uses the
   first two.
2. Component 1 measures **how much a customer buys**: r = 0.69 with log total quantity (Spearman 0.88) and r = 0.89
   with the number of distinct products bought. All 3,687 product loadings share one sign, so it is a size axis,
   not a taste axis.
3. Component 2 is unrelated to size (r = -0.02 with log quantity). It contrasts the heart/wicker/T-light home-decor
   range (White Hanging Heart T-Light Holder, Heart Of Wicker Small, Assorted Colour Bird Ornament) with the
   children's and lunch-box range (Lunch Bag Spaceboy Design, Round Snack Boxes Set Of4 Woodland, Plasters In Tin
   Spaceboy).
4. Because component 1 is so skewed, k-means on the raw pair puts 12 enormous accounts in their own group. The map
   therefore writes the same two numbers as **size** (log10 distance from the origin) and **mix** (angle). k-means
   (4 groups, 20 restarts, seed 42) runs on these two standardised values, fitted on training data only. This is
   clustering on two features, not on all 100 components. The map shows every customer at full range. The
   supplementary figure plots the literal component 1 and 2 scores with the same labels, as direct evidence of the
   two-component projection.
5. Each group is named from evidence: a volume tier (its median units against the all-customer median of 455) plus
   the theme of its most *distinctive* products. "Mix lift" = the product's share of the group's purchases divided
   by its share of all purchases. This corrects for big buyers simply buying more of everything. A theme is used
   only when its keywords match at least half of the ten distinctive products; otherwise the group is "mixed gifts".

## What marketing could do with them

* **High-volume wholesalers** (a quarter of customers, two thirds of all units bought): protect and grow. They
  reorder often, across a very wide range. Campaigns: early access to new children's and lunch-box ranges, volume
  pricing. This group and the smaller children's-range group have the largest overseas share (about 14%, mostly
  Germany and France).
* **Mid-size home-decor buyers** (the largest group): seasonal home-decor launches (Easter, Christmas hanging
  decorations), heart and wicker collections, furniture pieces.
* **Smaller children's-range and party buyers**: few orders so far (2.3 on average). Campaigns to win a second
  and third order: children's gifts, party supplies and baking sets.
* **Occasional small buyers**: about 1.6 orders each and little overlap between them; even their most common
  product reaches only 7% of members. Their distinctive products are a mix of gifts (parasols, tea glasses,
  teacups, a candle, a fan), so treat this as a reactivation group rather than a taste group.

## Limits to tell Sarah

* The map is one continuous cloud, not separate islands. Boundaries are a planning convenience, and customers
  near a boundary could sit in either group.
* Two components summarise only part of what each customer buys. The groups describe tendencies (mix lift
  1.6 to 5.2), not exclusive tastes.
* 91% of customers are UK based and every group is mainly UK (85.5% to 97.7%). The overseas share does differ
  (about 14% in the two children's-range groups, 3.3% and 2.3% in the other two), but country is not what defines the
  groups, so the names deliberately avoid geography.
* The groups are built from purchases up to October 2011. Re-run the segmentation as new data arrives, and test
  any group-specific campaign against a control before rolling it out.
